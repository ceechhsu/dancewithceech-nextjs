The Pimp Walk dance is an established move in the locking vocabulary. It combines kick-and-hop footwork with upper-body gestures that help the dancer create a character and connect with the audience.

I learned this move directly from Skeeter Rabbit. My first reaction was that its lower-body action felt exactly like the Scooby Walk. What separates the two most clearly is the upper body: the Scooby Walk uses a more specific uppercut-like arm swing, while the Pimp Walk leaves more room for expressive gestures.

## What is the Pimp Walk in locking?

In locking, the Pimp Walk is a rhythmic kick-and-hop step performed with character. The feet establish the groove and weight changes. The arms, hands, face, and focus give the movement its personality.

The name refers to this particular locking step. It should not be confused with unrelated memes, television scenes, line dances, or general descriptions of somebody walking with swagger.

The movement is not a fixed pantomime that every dancer must copy exactly. The lower-body rhythm identifies the step, while the upper body gives you space to improvise and make the character your own.

## Who created the Pimp Walk?

[LockerLegends’ record of locking steps](https://www.lockerlegends.net/the-steps-moves-of-locking/) attributes the Pimp Walk to Greg “Campbellock Jr.” Pope and describes it as very similar to the Scooby Walk.

Pope was one of the seven original members of The Lockers. [The Lockers’ official history](https://www.thelockersdance.com/about) identifies him as an original member of the group that performed on television as The Lockers beginning in 1973. It also credits the members’ individual improvisational contributions with helping introduce the dance to wider audiences.

An earlier version of this article connected the Pimp Walk directly to zoot-suit culture in 1940s Harlem and credited Don Campbell and The Lockers collectively with codifying the step. I could not verify that direct lineage. The more specific locking source credits Greg Pope, so this guide follows that attribution.

## Is the Pimp Walk the same as the Scooby Walk?

The Pimp Walk and Scooby Walk are closely related, but they are not presented as the same complete move.

I learned the Pimp Walk directly from Skeeter Rabbit, and the lower-body pattern immediately reminded me of the Scooby Walk. LockerLegends independently describes the two moves as very similar.

The practical distinction is in the upper body:

- **Scooby Walk:** The arms swing in an uppercut-like action.
- **Pimp Walk:** The arms create a charming, conversational character through gestures such as waving or blowing a kiss.

You can study the shared foundation in my [complete Scooby Walk tutorial](https://youtu.be/odoaSlOywD8). Learn that lower-body relationship first. It is more useful than trying to memorize every gesture Flo Master performs in the shorter Pimp Walk demonstration.

## How do you do the Pimp Walk dance?

Use the video as the full visual instruction. The written version should help you notice the important parts without pretending that prose can replace a moving demonstration.

### 1. Find the rhythm before making the movement large

Count:

**1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and**

There is movement on every “and.” If you hear only 1, 2, 3, and 4, you will miss the rhythm between the main beats.

Start by marking the timing with small weight changes. Keep listening instead of rushing to perform the complete move.

### 2. Study the kick-and-hop action

Watch only the demonstrators’ legs. Notice the kick, the hop, and how the body transfers weight before the next action.

Keep the movement small while learning. A smaller kick and hop make it easier to stay balanced and hear the timing.

### 3. Repeat the lower body without copying the arms

Practice the footwork until you can maintain the repeated count without stopping to remember what comes next.

This is where the Scooby Walk relationship can help. If you already know the Scooby Walk, use that familiar lower-body feeling as your reference.

### 4. Add simple gestures

Once the feet stay in rhythm, add one uncomplicated upper-body idea. You might wave, acknowledge someone, or direct your focus toward an imaginary person in the room.

Do not reproduce every gesture from the demonstration as if it were a required sequence. The goal is to understand the character and respond naturally to the music.

### 5. Let the complete move breathe

Relax your shoulders and face. Allow the gestures to sit on top of the lower-body rhythm instead of pulling your attention away from it.

If adding the arms makes you lose the beat or your balance, remove them and return to the feet.

## Why do beginners lose rhythm and balance?

The Pimp Walk can feel complicated because the feet, rhythm, balance, and character all ask for attention at the same time.

The most common problem is not a lack of effort. It is trying too hard to remember the complete move. When a beginner concentrates on every detail at once, they often stop listening to the music. Their weight transfer becomes uncertain, and their balance goes with it.

Use this order instead:

1. Hear **1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and**.
2. Mark the kick and hop at a manageable size.
3. Maintain the lower-body pattern without stopping.
4. Add one simple gesture.
5. Add more personality only when the rhythm and balance remain stable.

## Common Pimp Walk corrections

| What you notice | Likely issue | What to correct |
|---|---|---|
| You keep falling behind the music | You are focusing only on the numbered beats | Count every “and” and mark the in-between movements |
| You lose your balance during the hop | The kick or weight change is too large | Reduce the range and make the landing controlled |
| The feet work until you add the arms | You are coordinating too many details at once | Remove the arms, recover the rhythm, then add one gesture |
| Your arms look rehearsed or stiff | You are copying the demonstration literally | Use the gestures as examples and respond to the music |
| You cannot repeat the move continuously | You are treating it as disconnected poses | Keep the weight moving through the kick-and-hop pattern |
| The Pimp Walk looks exactly like your Scooby Walk | You are using the same upper-body action | Replace the uppercut-like swing with freer character gestures |

## Is the Pimp Walk difficult for beginners?

The individual kick and hop are not especially difficult for many dancers. The challenge is maintaining rhythm and balance while adding the upper-body character.

Complete beginners should practice the movement in layers rather than attempting the polished version immediately. Do not measure progress by how closely you can imitate every detail in the video. First ask whether you can keep the count and remain balanced.

## Is the Pimp Walk safe to practice?

The Pimp Walk does not require floorwork or extreme flexibility, but it does include kicking and hopping.

If you have a medical issue, pain, a balance limitation, or difficulty hopping, avoid the complete move and consult an appropriate healthcare professional when necessary. Do not force the hop through discomfort.

Clear enough space to kick without striking furniture or another person. Practice at a manageable speed and wear secure footwear on a stable surface.

## What should you practice next?

Build your Pimp Walk in this order:

1. Listen for the numbered beats and every “and.”
2. Practice the kick-and-hop pattern at a small range.
3. Stabilize your weight transfer and balance.
4. Compare the lower body with the Scooby Walk.
5. Add one simple upper-body gesture.
6. Use your own character instead of copying every detail.
